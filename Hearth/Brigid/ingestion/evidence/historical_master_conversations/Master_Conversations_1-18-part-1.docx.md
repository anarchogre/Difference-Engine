Explain the behavioral models: FATE Model, Six-Axis Model of Influence, and Authority Triangle.

Those three models are not established, universally accepted models in psychology or psychiatry. Instead, they are practical frameworks that have emerged in fields like negotiation, leadership, intelligence analysis, executive protection, behavior prediction, and influence operations. They can be useful as thinking tools, but they should not be confused with evidence-based clinical models.

Here's how each one works.

---

1. FATE Model

FATE is a way to understand why someone acts.

The letters typically stand for:

F – Fear

A – Attachment (or Ambition, depending on the version)

T – Territory (or Threat)

E – Ego

The idea is that nearly every significant behavior is driven primarily by one or more of these four forces.

Fear

People act to avoid pain, embarrassment, uncertainty, punishment, or loss.

Examples:

avoiding homework because failure feels overwhelming

lying to avoid consequences

withdrawing from social situations

With children: A child refusing school might not be "defiant." Fear may be driving the behavior.

---

Attachment

Humans protect what they value.

Examples:

family

favorite toys

routines

hobbies

reputation

friendships

Children with autism often develop particularly strong attachments to:

routines

special interests

familiar people

predictable environments

Threatening those attachments often increases distress.

---

Territory

Territory means anything someone considers "mine."

Examples:

bedroom

personal space

control over choices

schedule

belongings

identity

For many children, especially those with ADHD or autistic traits, loss of autonomy can be experienced as a major threat.

Instead of saying:

> "Put your shoes on now."

Offering a choice:

> "Blue shoes or black shoes?"

helps preserve their sense of territory and control.

---

Ego

Everyone wants to feel:

competent

respected

understood

important

Children are especially sensitive to:

public correction

embarrassment

unfair comparisons

feeling "bad"

Protecting a child's sense of competence often reduces conflict.

---

Using FATE

Suppose a 7-year-old refuses to leave the playground.

Instead of assuming stubbornness, ask:

Fear?

afraid fun is ending?

Attachment?

attached to current activity?

Territory?

feels forced?

Ego?

doesn't want to be seen "losing?"

Often several overlap.

---

2. Six-Axis Model of Influence

This is a more comprehensive way to analyze why someone is likely to comply, resist, or change behavior.

Different versions exist, but a common interpretation includes these six dimensions:

1. Motivation

What does the person actually want?

Examples:

fun

safety

praise

money

independence

---

2. Capability

Can they actually do what is being asked?

Sometimes noncompliance is simply inability.

Examples:

ADHD working-memory problems

autism processing delays

fatigue

---

3. Opportunity

Does the environment support success?

Example:

A child can't focus because:

TV is on

siblings are loud

lights are harsh

The environment—not motivation—is the problem.

---

4. Social Influence

Who affects them?

Examples:

parents

teachers

friends

YouTubers

siblings

Children often imitate whoever has the strongest emotional connection.

---

5. Consequences

What happens after the behavior?

Behavior followed by:

praise

relief

attention

rewards

is more likely to repeat.

---

6. Identity

People behave consistently with who they believe they are.

Examples:

"I am helpful."

"I am smart."

"I am the funny kid."

"I hate math."

Children often internalize repeated labels.

Instead of:

"You're so stubborn."

Try:

"I know you're someone who solves hard problems."

That reinforces a more constructive identity.

---

3. Authority Triangle

The Authority Triangle describes three foundations of effective authority. Authority is strongest when all three are present.

Competence

Does this person know what they're doing?

Children notice this too.

If a teacher gives confusing instructions, their authority may weaken.

---

Legitimacy

Does this person have the right to direct me?

Examples:

parent

teacher

coach

police officer

Legitimacy comes from role and accepted responsibility.

---

Trust (or Relationship)

Does the person genuinely care about me?

Without trust, even highly competent authority figures may face resistance.

---

Visualizing the triangle

Competence

/ \

Trust ---------------- Legitimacy

If one side weakens, authority becomes less stable.

For example:

High competence + legitimacy, but low trust:

"He knows what he's doing, but I don't like him."

High trust + competence, but low legitimacy:

A favorite uncle may be listened to, but cannot enforce bedtime like a parent can.

High legitimacy + trust, but low competence:

A caring teacher who struggles to explain the material.

The strongest authority combines all three.

---

Applying these models to a 7-year-old with ADHD/autistic traits

Taken together, these frameworks encourage looking beneath the surface of behavior rather than assuming a child is simply "being difficult."

For example, if the child refuses to start homework:

FATE: Is the main driver fear of failure, attachment to a current activity, loss of control, or protecting self-esteem?

Six-Axis Model: Does the child want to succeed but lack the executive-function skills, environmental support, or confidence to begin?

Authority Triangle: Does the adult giving the instruction have a trusting relationship with the child, communicate competence, and have accepted authority?

This perspective aligns well with current approaches to neurodivergent children, which emphasize identifying unmet needs, reducing unnecessary demands, supporting executive functioning, and building collaborative relationships rather than relying solely on compliance or punishment. While these models themselves are not standard clinical frameworks, they can be useful lenses for understanding behavior when combined with evidence-based knowledge about ADHD, autism, and child development.

Logical reasoning

Reading comprehension

Spatial visualization

Mathematical thinking

Long-term memory for preferred interests

Beginning tasks

Finishing tasks

Organizing materials

Transitioning between activities

Regulating emotions

Delaying gratification

Maintaining attention on uninteresting work

Children rarely develop every skill at exactly the same pace.

intellectual abilities may resemble those of an older child,

emotional regulation may remain age-typical or younger,

organizational skills may lag significantly,

It does not.

eat healthier,

save money,

sleep earlier,

spend less time on phones.

remain focused,

resist distractions,

remember multiple steps,

regulate impulses,

monitor progress,

recognize mistakes,

shift flexibly between activities,

finish what has been started.

These abilities continue developing well into young adulthood.

"He Can Do It When He Wants To."

Among parents, teachers, and even experienced professionals, one of the most common observations is:

> "He can do it perfectly when it's something he enjoys."

At first glance, this appears to prove that the child is choosing not to perform during less interesting activities. After all, if he can focus on chess for an hour, why can't he spend ten minutes putting away laundry?

The distinction is subtle but extremely important.

Chess

Building projects

Reading favorite books

Video games

Creative stories

Science topics

Collecting facts

Adults often attach moral judgments to motivation.

Words such as:

lazy

stubborn

spoiled

defiant

manipulative

Motivation depends on the interaction between the environment and the brain.

Tasks that feel immediately rewarding naturally receive more attention than tasks that offer delayed rewards.

Children vary tremendously in how much outside structure they require before they can persist through low-interest activities.

Understanding this difference does not remove accountability.

Instead, it changes how accountability is taught.

Rather than assuming the child simply refuses to comply, adults begin asking:

"What supports would help this child succeed consistently?"

That question often leads to much more productive solutions.

Dopamine and Everyday Behavior

Dopamine is often described as the brain's "reward chemical," but that description is incomplete.

More accurately, dopamine helps determine what the brain considers worth pursuing.

Activities that are exciting, novel, challenging, surprising, or emotionally meaningful tend to activate motivational pathways more strongly than repetitive routines.

For many children, brushing teeth simply cannot compete with an exciting chess game.

This does not mean hygiene is unimportant.

It means the brain naturally assigns different levels of urgency.

Some children can override those preferences with relatively little effort.

Others require significantly more external structure until those internal control systems mature.
